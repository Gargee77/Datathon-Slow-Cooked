Half the world's calories cross a border. Nobody checks what's in them

Pooled across 178 national food supplies, latest year. Home-grown calories win on four of five micronutrients. Only B12 goes the other way, because meat and dairy travel too.

Why. Anemia in women of reproductive age is common, and it's getting worse. It rose in 123 of 162 countries since 2010. Two reasons this is a food-supply problem, not just a health statistic. One: the data measures food available per person, nationally. That average can look fine on paper while women don't get their share of it. They're more food-insecure than men in 98 of 135 countries. Two: the average doesn't adjust for biology. Iron needs peak during the exact reproductive years this analysis covers.

What. Which countries have traded their way into a starch-rich, micronutrient-poor food supply, and does it show up in women's anemia? The reasoning: starch, sugar and oil ship and store well; vegetables, eggs and fish don't, so trade quietly selects for calories over nutrition without anyone deciding it. That's a clean, testable chain. We tested it four ways. It broke.

What we assumed

Some of this is already caveated elsewhere on this page; some of it isn't stated anywhere else. Collected in one place, before any of the findings below:

Supply is not intake

FAO data is national per-capita food supply, not what women actually eat, and it cannot see who gets what inside a household.

Associations, not causes

The starch to anemia link is cross-country. The within-country and lagged tests found nothing, so this shows who's at risk today, not what would fix it tomorrow.

Missing countries

FAO balance sheets omit several of the worst: Mali 56.5%, Benin 50.0%, Chad 46.5%, Togo 41.5%, Burundi 39.0%. Every relationship here is likely a floor.

A modelled outcome

The World Bank anemia series is partly model-interpolated between surveys, which blunts year-to-year signal. DHS measured anemia covers 49 countries.

A hand-built crosswalk

99 FBS item codes were mapped to 15 SUA food groups by the team, not by FAO. Every decision is documented in the pipeline doc.

Many tests

Around nine variables were tried. Malaria and starch are large enough to be unlikely flukes, but they deserve held-out validation.

Each country's latest year

Countries are compared at whatever year is most recently available for them, not one shared year. A 2023 figure sits next to another country's 2015 figure.

"Trapped" is a line we drew

The starch trap is the riskiest third of countries on imports, the riskiest third on starch, and the bottom third on micronutrients. Draw the cutoff elsewhere and the list changes.

The order we tested in

Rival explanations were ruled out income first, then malaria, water, fertility and iron supplementation, in that order. A different order could shift how much credit each one gets.

Two modelling choices

Income enters as its logarithm, assuming an extra dollar matters more to a poor country than a rich one. And every effect is treated as a straight line that adds up on its own, with nothing amplifying or dampening anything else.

02 · Testing the premise

Before blaming trade, we checked whether the premise was even true

The question assumes two things: that half the world's calories now cross a border, and that the traveling half is the less nourishing one. Both are testable, and neither had been checked on this data. So that is where the analysis starts.

Across 178 countries we divided imported calories by total calories supplied, then averaged country by country, year by year. The share rose from 43.0% in 2010 to 50.4% in 2023, about +0.64 points a year, without a single reversal (r = 0.98). The average national diet crossed the halfway line in 2020.

Share of dietary calories that are imported
Unweighted mean across 178 countries, 2010 to 2023. The dashed line is the halfway mark.

Weighting by population gives the same direction, +0.40 points a year, while population-weighted starch share actually fell by 0.14 points a year. More trade has not meant starchier diets on its own. The damage is where the two overlap.

The second claim holds too, and for a mundane reason: the world ships what keeps. Sugar, oil and grain survive a month at sea. Leafy greens and eggs do not.

How traded each food group is, and how much iron it carries
Sorted by import share. Two panels, each on its own scale, so the exact value is always on the bar. Hover a row for its calorie and iron shares.
Import share of the group's caloriesIron, mg per 1,000 kcal

Cereals, oils and sugar are 60% of the average country's calories but only 37% of its iron. Vegetables are 2% of calories and deliver 13% of the iron and 26% of the vitamin A.

No FAO file labels a calorie as imported and then reports its nutrients, so that measure had to be built. Each food group's calories and nutrients get split by that group's own import share, the pieces are summed, and the two halves are compared per 1,000 kcal against a woman's daily requirement.

Density gap: domestic minus imported nutrient density
Number of countries per 20-point bin, measured as percent of daily requirement delivered per 1,000 kcal.
Home-grown denserImported denser, for example Argentina and the Netherlands

146 of 178 countries show a positive gap, median +33% of daily need per 1,000 kcal. Protein says the same: 26.7 g per 1,000 kcal imported against 32.9 g home-grown, thinner in 131 of 177 countries. By starch it is stronger still, with imported calories starchier in 84% of countries.

So the premise is real. Trade has pushed half the world's plate across a border, and what crosses is the thinner half. But trade alone doesn't make a diet starchy. The next test makes that explicit.

Import dependence does not predict anemia

Import dependence does not predict anemia in women. Not across countries, not within a single country tracked over thirteen years, and not three years ahead of time. The correlation is −0.18, and the sign points the wrong way: countries buying the most food from abroad trend slightly healthier.

The most import-dependent economies on earth are Belgium and the Netherlands. The starchiest diet belongs to DR Congo, which imports 7.2% of its food and grows the rest. Starch and trade are negatively correlated globally. The countries eating the most starch are the ones trading least.

If trade were driving this, the countries buying the most food would be the sickest. They are the healthiest. Trade dependence is nutritionally safe when a country is rich enough to also import variety, and risky only where imports reinforce a diet that was already starch-heavy.

We are publishing this because it is the finding, and because a reader who can't see what we ruled out has no way to weigh what we kept.

03 · Who is trapped

Twelve countries have traded their way into a starch trap

We define the starch trap as a country that is simultaneously in the top third for imported calories, the top third for starch share, and the bottom third for micronutrient supply.

Each condition on its own points at the wrong countries. High imports alone catches Macao, the Netherlands and Belgium, who buy a diverse, low-starch diet from abroad. High starch alone catches DR Congo, 83% starch and 7% imported, which grows its own staples, so trade is not the route. Only the intersection isolates the countries the question is actually about.

04 · What it costs women

Median anemia is 32.8% inside the trap and 24.7% outside it

Why this is a women's story

National food supply is an average, and women often do not receive the average. Iron needs peak during the reproductive years, nutrient-dense foods cost more, and women are more likely to go without them.

98/135countries where women are more food-insecure than men (73%)
1.8×cost of animal-source foods vs starchy staples (vegetables 1.4×); unaffordability of a healthy diet correlates with anemia at r = +0.63
123/162countries where anemia in women rose between 2010 and 2023 (mean 25.2% to 27.2%)

Iron on the label is not iron in the blood

Niger's food supply delivers 24.2 mg of iron per person per day, above the commonly cited 18 mg target, yet 47% of its women are anemic. The reason is absorption, not supply: iron from meat, fish and eggs is absorbed efficiently, iron from cereals and beans largely is not, and only 4% of Niger's iron comes from animal sources. Across countries, total iron supply barely tracks anemia at r = −0.21, while the animal-source share tracks it 2.7 times more strongly at r = −0.55.

CountryIron supplied, mg/dayFrom animal sourcesAnemia, womenGDP per person

Guatemala is one fifth as rich as Australia and has lower anemia. Income is not destiny. Between 2010 and 2023 the typical country's iron supply rose from 15.4 to 16.0 mg a day, and rose in 102 of 162 countries. Anemia rose anyway in 73 of those 102. Burkina Faso, at 24.8 mg a day and 44% anemia, matches Niger's pattern.

Women receive less than their share of an already thin food supply, and the iron in it is the kind their bodies absorb worst. Global anemia in women is also moving the wrong way, so this is not a problem solving itself.
05 · What held up

Nine explanations were tested. Four held, five collapsed

Across three notebooks and fifteen scripts the team tried roughly nine explanations for why anemia sits where it does. Keeping the failures visible is the point: the ones that collapsed are what rules out the easy story.

Some of the worst burdens sit outside the trap altogether. India has 53.7% anemia and imports only 6.8% of its calories, mostly edible oils, sugar and pulses, even though it is the world's largest vegetable-oil importer. Gabon has the worst rate in the panel at 59.9%, on $18,700 per person, with imports below the threshold. Their drivers are domestic diets, malaria, sanitation and health access, not trade.

06 · Where this is heading

Fixing the diet did not fix the anemia, and growth did not fix either

If diet were a quick lever, a trap country cutting its starch should watch anemia fall. Two trap countries moved in opposite directions, and they show why the within-country test came up empty.

Lesotho

Starch share falling 1.04 points a year, the fastest diet improvement of any trap country.

Anemia rising 0.81 points a year anyway. 34.2% today, 39.4% forecast by 2030.

Gambia

Starch share falling only 0.17 points a year, barely moving at all.

The only trap country where anemia is falling, down 0.68 points a year. 46.1% today, 41.9% by 2030.

Getting richer is not an exit from the trap

Income is falling in several of the countries flagged here, most sharply Afghanistan, where GDP per person dropped by nearly a third. But growth is no escape either: Bhutan and Botswana both grew fast and are both still in the trap.

CountryGroupGDP per person, 20102023Change
Trade is not the villain. It is the amplifier. Importing is safe when a country can afford to import diversity. It becomes a trap where imports reinforce a plate that was already starchy.

We built a forecast, back-tested it, and replaced it with a watch list

Fitting each country's 2010 to 2023 line and extending it to 2030 named Haiti and Cabo Verde as new trap countries. Then the back-test ran: fit up to 2016, 2017 and so on, predict the years after, compare. The straight-line trend did worse than assuming nothing changes at all, 4.45 points of error against 3.73 on import share.

The model that survived damps the trend to 10% of its slope. Under it no country confidently crosses by 2030, and the uncertainty is about ±6.7 points on imports and ±4.4 on starch. So rather than name winners, the analysis measures how far each country sits from its last unmet condition, in cross-country standard deviations.

CountryDistance to trapStill missingConditions metObserved drift

"Closes in about N years" is the remaining gap divided by observed drift, where that drift is statistically significant. That is arithmetic on history, not a validated forecast. A wider lens finds 47 countries moving toward the trap on both fronts at once, including Samoa, Papua New Guinea, the Philippines and Namibia.

Can trade composition serve as an early warning? As a screen, yes, it reliably flags who is structurally exposed. As a timer, no. Shifts in a country's import mix do not precede shifts in its anemia. Use it to decide where to look, then track anemia directly.

The price case for acting now

Trap countries buy most of their grain abroad, so world prices reach them through the import bill. If the last decade repeats, wheat rises 17 to 57% by 2030 depending on grade and method, maize 20 to 41%, rice 14 to 19%. Chicken is the exception, falling about 14%, which would make one well-absorbed source of iron cheaper.

Global export prices in US dollars, not adjusted for inflation, projected from their 2023 to 2025 average. These show where prices go if the last decade continues; they are not forecasts of what any country will pay. Local staple prices are calmer today, falling in six of the eight trap and watch-list countries with current data, and prices showed no measurable link to anemia. Read this as budget pressure on import-dependent governments, not as a cause.

Six things that would actually help

1
Governments of trap countries · port and standards authorities
Fortify the imported staples at the border

Make iron, folic acid and vitamin A fortification a condition of import for wheat flour, rice, maize and edible oil, which are the exact calories trap countries import most. The imported half then delivers micronutrients instead of diluting them.

Supply is concentrated enough for this to work: the top three exporters ship 68 to 78% of traded rice, 61 to 66% of maize and 83 to 86% of palm oil. First movers would be Gambia and Liberia, where rice is about 100% imported, Lesotho on maize and wheat, and Djibouti on nearly everything.

2
Trade and finance ministries · public procurement
Rebalance the import basket toward iron-rich food

Cut tariffs and VAT on pulses, dried fish, eggs and milk powder, and use state procurement and food-aid contracts to buy them rather than only grain. Spread purchases across suppliers so one shock cannot empty the shelf.

Chicken is the one iron-rich import projected to get cheaper, about 14% by 2030. Watch concentration risk: 78% of traded lentils come from three exporters.

3
Agriculture ministries · development partners
Invest in home-grown nutrition

Fund home and school gardens, cold chains and extension services for vegetables, pulses, small livestock and eggs, and screen trade policy so cheap imports do not crowd out local crops.

For Morocco, Cabo Verde and Haiti, this is the way to hold the last unmet threshold.

4
Health and social-protection ministries · NGOs
Put women 15 to 49 first, directly

National averages hide who goes without. Reach women directly with weekly iron and folic acid supplementation through antenatal care, schools and workplaces, and add cash or vouchers earmarked for eggs, fish, meat and pulses.

Measure intake by sex, not by household.

5
FAO · WHO · WFP · national statistics offices
Run the starch-trap screen every year

The three conditions can be recomputed annually from the Food Balance Sheets FAO already publishes. Flag any country within 0.25 standard deviations of its last threshold for a nutrition review: today that is Morocco, Cabo Verde, Haiti and El Salvador.

Pair the screen with the anchored anemia forecast, and confirm the timing with anemia data itself.

6
Health ministries · researchers
Treat the non-food causes, and close the data gaps

Where anemia is high but trade is not the route, pair nutrition work with malaria control and with water and sanitation.

Split anemia by cause with IHME GBD data, pull a malaria time series rather than a single year, and study fortification mandates as a natural experiment.

08 · Method, sources & limits

Every number above traces to a file. Here is what to read before citing one

What this analysis cannot tell you

The core assumptions behind every number on this page are stated up front, in "Why this, what we're asking." What follows here is the remaining chart-specific fine print.

The density gap is compositional

It reflects which food groups get traded, not whether imported wheat is worse than local wheat. Vitamins A and C dominate it.

Malaria is a single year

2021 only, and missing for five trap countries, so it explains differences between countries and not change over time.

Price projections are extrapolations

World US-dollar prices, not adjusted for inflation, and not what any country actually pays.

The method, in the order it actually happened

Every FAOSTAT file has the same shape: country, item, element, year, value. One row is one fact. We joined five sources on country and year, then ran the same test repeatedly while adding rival explanations one at a time.

167countries in the fitted model
0.463R squared, so 46% of the variation explained
5.435z score on the starch coefficient, p below 0.001
5rival explanations added one at a time
The model, written out

anemia% = 40.02 + 5.07 × import share + 31.98 × starch share + 0.49 × micronutrient adequacy − 3.15 × log(GDP per capita). Fitted on 167 countries. R² = 0.463. The model explains 46% of the variation; the rest is healthcare, genetics, fortification and geography. South Asia alone carries 11 extra points that income and diet together can't account for. The starch coefficient has z = 5.435 and p < 0.001, with a confidence interval of 20.45 to 43.52, nowhere near zero.

Why we controlled for income first

Income correlates with starch share at −0.759. Richer countries eat less starch and have less anemia, so any raw starch-anemia relationship could simply be poverty wearing a costume. Holding income constant was the first thing we did, not the last, before adding malaria, water, fertility or iron supplementation.

Why we then tested within countries over time

Comparing different countries risks confusing "starchy" with "poor" or "African" or "rural". Panel fixed effects compare each country only against itself, cancelling every permanent trait. Nothing survived that test. The honest reading is that the relationship is structural rather than a lever: it tells you who is at risk today, not that changing diet tomorrow would fix it.

The full correlation picture, since the obvious chain does not run straight

More imports, starchier diet, more anemia: that chain is the intuition this project started from, and the matrix is where it visibly breaks. Import share is negatively linked to starch share and barely linked to anemia at all. Starch share is the variable that tracks anemia, though it is tangled with income.

Pearson r, 2023 snapshot, 178 countries. Warm is positive, cool is negative, and intensity is strength. Sources and method notes sit in "Method, sources & limits," above.
