01 · The question

Half the world's calories cross a border. Nobody checks what's in them

We started with a specific question: which countries have traded their way into a starch-rich, micronutrient-poor food supply, and does it show up in women's anemia?

The logic seemed sound. Starch, sugar and oil ship well and don't spoil. Vegetables, eggs and fish don't. So the global food trade selects, relentlessly and without anyone deciding it, for calories over nutrition. Cereals, oil and sugar alone are 57% of the average person's diet. If trade pushes diets toward starch, and starch-heavy diets are low in absorbable iron, then women in trade-dependent countries should be more anemic.

That is a clean, testable chain. We tested it four separate ways, and it broke.

03 · What we got wrong

We came looking for a trade story. The data refused

Import dependence does not predict anemia in women. Not across countries, not within a single country tracked over thirteen years, and not three years ahead of time. The correlation is −0.18, and the sign points the wrong way: countries buying the most food from abroad trend slightly healthier.

The most import-dependent economies on earth are Belgium and the Netherlands. The starchiest diet belongs to DR Congo, which imports 7.2% of its food and grows the rest. Starch and trade are negatively correlated globally. The countries eating the most starch are the ones trading least.

0.184correlation between import dependence and anemia, and it points the wrong way
7.2%of DR Congo's food is imported, and it has the starchiest diet on earth
98%of Belgium's food is imported, and its women are among the least anemic
If trade were driving this, the countries buying the most food would be the sickest. They are the healthiest. Trade dependence is nutritionally safe when a country is rich enough to also import variety, and risky only where imports reinforce a diet that was already starch-heavy.

We are publishing this because it is the finding, and because a reader who can't see what we ruled out has no way to weigh what we kept.

04 · What held

You can hit the iron target and still be anemic

Niger supplies 24.2 mg of iron per person per day. The requirement is 18. On paper Niger is fed. 47% of its women are anemic.

Because 81% of that iron comes from cereals and beans, where the body absorbs very little of it. Iron from meat, fish and eggs is absorbed several times more efficiently, and across all countries the share of iron from animal sources predicts anemia far better than the total does. The label counts milligrams. The body counts what it can take up.

The label counts milligrams. The body counts what it can actually take up. Iron from meat, fish and eggs absorbs several times more efficiently than iron from grain, which is why the share of iron from animal sources predicts anemia far better than the total ever does.

So we changed the question, from where the food came from to what it was made of. Starch share of diet is correlated with anemia at +0.315 across countries, a real, statistically significant relationship. We spent the rest of our time trying to explain it away.

05 · How we did it

The method, in the order it actually happened

Every FAOSTAT file has the same shape: country, item, element, year, value. One row is one fact. We joined five sources on country and year, then ran the same test repeatedly while adding rival explanations one at a time.

167countries in the fitted model
0.463R squared, so 46% of the variation explained
5.435z score on the starch coefficient, p below 0.001
5rival explanations added one at a time
The model, written out

anemia% = 40.02 + 5.07 × import share + 31.98 × starch share + 0.49 × micronutrient adequacy − 3.15 × log(GDP per capita). Fitted on 167 countries. R² = 0.463. The starch coefficient has z = 5.435 and p < 0.001, with a confidence interval of 20.45 to 43.52, nowhere near zero.

Why we controlled for income first

Income correlates with starch share at −0.759. Richer countries eat less starch and have less anemia, so any raw starch-anemia relationship could simply be poverty wearing a costume. Holding income constant was the first thing we did, not the last.

Why we then tested within countries over time

Comparing different countries risks confusing “starchy” with “poor” or “African” or “rural”. Panel fixed effects compare each country only against itself, cancelling every permanent trait. Nothing survived that test. The honest reading is that the relationship is structural rather than a lever: it tells you who is at risk today, not that changing diet tomorrow would fix it.

Why iron quantity isn't the answer

Raw iron supply in milligrams per day does not predict anemia once income is controlled (p = 0.75). It cannot distinguish heme iron from meat, which absorbs readily, from non-heme iron in cereals, which largely doesn't. Composition beats totals.

What we did about missing data

Twenty countries are absent from the FAO data entirely, including Mali, Chad and Somalia. Six more have no World Bank anemia figure. We report on what remains and say so rather than imputing. Since the missing countries carry some of the world's worst anemia, our estimates are likely conservative.

07 · Limits

What this cannot tell you

This is national food supply, not what any individual woman ate. In many of these countries food is not shared equally inside a household, and we have no data that reaches the table.
Twenty countries are missing from the FAO data entirely, including Mali, Chad and Somalia, which carry some of the world's worst anemia. Six more have no World Bank anemia figure.
The relationship is structural, not a lever. Across countries, starchier diets go with more anemia. Track one country over thirteen years and its own diet changes do not move its own anemia.
The model explains 46% of the variation. The rest is healthcare, genetics, fortification and geography. South Asia alone carries 11 extra points that income and diet together cannot account for.
Anemia has causes that are not food. Malaria is a genuine independent predictor. It absorbs 14% of the starch effect and leaves the rest intact.
Nothing here is causal. Every relationship on this site is an association, measured carefully and reported with its controls.
